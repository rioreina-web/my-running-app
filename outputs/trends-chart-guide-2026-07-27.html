POST RUN DRIP TRENDS AUDIT 12 CHARTS JUL 27 2026

What every chart on
Trends is actually asking.

A plain-language guide to all twelve, in the order you meet them. For each one: what it wants to tell you, how to read it, and what you have to already know before it makes any sense. No changes have been made to the app. This is just so you can decide what earns its place.

The standard you already set

Your own IA doc describes the Trends tab in four words: the 5-second view.

Twelve charts is not a 5-second view. But the useful thing is that you already know what good looks like here, so this isn't a question of taste. It's a question of which charts clear the bar you set.

One more line of yours, written into SharpEndFitnessRead.swift yesterday: The shipped charts present data but never state a conclusion, so every read is homework. That is the exact diagnosis. Hold onto it while you read the next twelve entries.

Group A · The Read card

Three charts behind one switch

These live inside the black “Are you getting fitter?” card at the top of the screen. A three-way segmented control swaps between them, so you only ever see one at a time. All three are drawn from the same set of key sessions.

Fig. 1 — effort-levelled pace over the block
CHART 01
Progress over time
The Read card, first tab
What it asks
Is my pace improving, once I account for how hard I was working on each day?
How to read it
Time runs left to right. The black line is your pace, and it is flipped so higher means faster. The dashed line is where you started. The grey band is normal week-to-week wobble: while the line sits inside it, nothing is proven.
What you must already know
That every session has been mathematically re-priced to “what would you have run at 162 bpm” so that a hard rep day and an easy tempo day can share one scale. If you don’t accept that step, the whole line is meaningless.
Verdict
Reads cleanly  The best chart on the surface. One line, one direction, and it states its conclusion in words underneath.
Fig. 2 — the two fitted lines, as they actually render
Look where they cross. Above the crossing the solid “now” line is to the left of the dashed one, which means slower. The caption under this chart in the app says “faster at every intensity.” The picture says otherwise.
CHART 02
The two lines
The Read card, second tab
What it asks
At any given effort level, am I holding a faster pace now than I was early in the block?
How to read it
Pace runs across, faster to the right. Heart rate runs up. Each dot is a session. The dashed line summarises early-you, the solid line summarises now-you. If the solid line sits entirely to the right of the dashed one, you are faster at every effort. The horizontal gap between them is the gain.
What you must already know
Linear regression. That a straight line can stand in for a cloud of dots. That horizontal distance between two lines at a fixed height is an effect size. This is a statistics-class chart.
Verdict
Broken  Not just hard, wrong. A handful of impossible data points (roughly 4:15/mi at 130 bpm, almost certainly a downhill segment or GPS artifact) flipped the slope of the fit. The lines now cross near 162 bpm, which is exactly where the app measures the gap. The reported number is the crossing point of two bad lines.
Fig. 3 — every session, raw
CHART 03
Every session
The Read card, third tab
What it asks
What does my raw data actually look like, before any fitting or adjusting?
How to read it
Each dot is one key session. Pace across (faster right), heart rate up. Bottom-right is the good corner: fast for a low heart rate. Bigger dot means more fast miles. More solid dot means more recent.
What you must already know
Three codes at once — position, size and opacity — and the legend only explains one of them. Nothing on screen tells you that dot size means volume or that faded means old.
Verdict
Hard work  Honest, but wordless. Its own caption explains how the chart is encoded rather than telling you anything about your training.
Group B · The compare maps

Two full-width scatter plots

Further down the same scroll, under the headings “THE MAP” and “WORKLOAD”. Both are always visible, and both have an expand button that opens the same chart bigger in a sheet.

Fig. 4 — pace × effort, A and B ringed
CHART 04
Pace × effort
Section “THE MAP”, always visible
What it asks
Of the two sessions I picked, which was faster for less effort — and where do they sit among everything else?
How to read it
Identical to chart 3. Pace across, heart rate up, bottom-right is fitter. The two ringed dots are your selected A and B.
What you must already know
Same as chart 3 — plus you have to notice that this one quietly uses raw heart rate while chart 3 uses heat-corrected heart rate, so the same session sits in two different places on two charts one scroll apart.
Verdict
Duplicate  This is chart 3 again, drawn 200 pixels lower with different rules. Two charts of the same two numbers, disagreeing slightly, is worse than either alone.
Fig. 5 — effort × volume
CHART 05
Effort × volume
Section “WORKLOAD”, always visible
What it asks
How hard was a session, relative to how much of it there was?
How to read it
Fast miles across, heart rate up. Top-left is short and sharp. Bottom-right is long and easy.
What you must already know
That average heart rate is standing in for “how hard”. That is the weak link: eight mile-reps with full recovery and a steady 6-mile tempo can produce the same average heart rate and are nothing like each other.
Verdict
Hard work  The premise is shakier than the drawing. It never says anything about your training; you have to supply the conclusion yourself.
Group C · The trend grid

Six small tiles, all on screen at once

A 2×3 grid under “TRENDS · TAP TO EXPAND”. Each tile is about a thumb tall and shows a title, a unit line and a bare line. Tapping opens a bigger version that adds the axis labels and one explanatory sentence — none of which are visible on the tile itself.

Fig. 6–11 — the grid as it appears in the app
CHARTS 06–11
Pace · Density · Fast volume · HR drift · Decoupling · Avg HR
Section “TRENDS · TAP TO EXPAND”
What they ask
Six separate questions: is my pace improving, am I running fast for more of the session, is my fast volume growing, is my heart rate staying flatter within a session, am I holding form on long efforts, and is the same work costing fewer beats?
The problem, in one sentence
Five of these six charts silently flip their vertical axis so that “up” always means “better”. The only signal is the tiny ▲ in the unit line. On the Fast volume tile, up means the number went up. On the five beside it, up means the number went down.
The second problem
The horizontal axis is session number, not date. Sessions are spaced evenly regardless of when they happened, so a three-week injury gap and a three-day gap look identical. The shape you are reading does not exist in time.
Verdict
Hard work  All six. Not because any one is badly drawn, but because reading the row requires holding six definitions and five axis inversions in your head simultaneously. Nobody can do that, including the person who built it.
WORKED EXAMPLE
Why the flip is so disorienting
HR drift tile — the real numbers, session by session:  +2   +5   +3   +8   +4   +6   +2
Your heart rate drifted more in session 4 than in session 1. That is worse. But the line on the tile dips down there, because the axis is inverted so that better is up.

Fast volume tile, right next to it:  5.0   3.0   4.2   6.0   3.5   4.8   6.2
Here the line goes up at session 4, and up means the number genuinely went up.

Same visual gesture, opposite meaning, nine pixels apart. That is the whole problem.
Group D · Fast segments

One chart, six datasets

A separate surface with a row of chips across the top (mile, 3k, 5k, 10k, HMP, MP). The chart redraws for whichever pace system you pick.

Fig. 12 — one pace system over its own sessions
CHART 12
5K · pace, heat + hills adjusted
Fast Segments, one chip selected
What it asks
For this one type of fast work, is it getting sharper?
How to read it
Your 5k-pace sessions in order, pace flipped so higher is faster. Colour deepens as pace gets faster.
What you must already know
That “5k” here means a pace, not a distance you raced. That the heat and hill corrections are trustworthy. And again, that the horizontal axis is session order, not calendar time.
Verdict
Reads cleanly  And here is the interesting part: it works because of the line of text sitting above it — “▼ 6s/mi faster than your first”. That sentence does all the work. Cover it up and the chart goes quiet. This is the pattern worth copying.

Two things I found in your own rules
Hard rule #8 — no em-dashes as empty-state placeholders

The head-to-head table prints “—” for every missing value, and with the sample data that is four of eleven rows: Decoupling, Heat cost, Hill cost, and Avg rest on continuous efforts. Your rule says every empty cell gets the empty-state component instead.

Worth noting this is the most common state for a new user. Someone in their first fortnight sees a table that is mostly dashes.

Three-palette rule — green is mood-only

Your colour rule from 2026-07-03 says blue is pace, warm is mood, coral is alert, and the three never share hues — with a specific note that a “safe zone” band goes neutral gray, never green.

Charts 1 and 2 both use a success green (#006300) for the “now” level, the gain bar and the “RIGHT = FITTER” label. That is a fourth palette doing a job the rules assign to ink weight. The head-to-head table already solves this correctly: bold ink for the stronger figure, gray for the weaker, no colour at all.

My earlier mockups used the same green. That was my error, and I would drop it to ink in any version we build.

If you only take one thing from this

The two charts that work (01 and 12) are the two that say a sentence first and show a picture second. The ten that don't are the ten that hand you a shape and trust you to derive the sentence yourself.

That's not a charting problem. It's the difference between a tool that observes for you and a tool that makes you do the observing — which is the entire premise of the product, printed at the top of your own CLAUDE.md: honest observation without prescription.

The observation part is the promise. Right now the Trends tab is outsourcing it back to the runner.
