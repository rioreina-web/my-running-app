Why this shape

Why volume is the only thing plotted

Capping the band at both ends means pace can only travel about 40 seconds. It stops being the noisy variable. Volume runs 1.2 to 6.0 miles across these sessions — a five-fold spread — so it is the thing with something to say, and the thing a runner can actually act on.

Why matched pairs instead of a trend line

Comparing a 1.2-mile session to a 6-mile one tells you nothing, which is what broke every earlier version of this chart. Matching on volume first means pace, heart rate and drift are being read like for like, and no regression is needed to say it.

Why heat sits beside the verdict, not in it

Heat cost runs from 0.1s at 54°F to 12s at 76°F in your data, so it is real and it is worth naming. But it is a condition, not a performance, so it explains the number rather than scoring it.

Where the good news actually is

Your two cleanest matched pairs both say the same thing. Mar 31 → Jul 8: same 6 miles, 3s slower, 4 beats lower, 8°F hotter. Apr 4 → Jul 21: same 6 miles, 6s slower, 3 beats lower, 7°F hotter. Slower in the heat at a materially lower cost is the shape of getting fitter through a summer.

What this sketch cannot do yet

Drift needs three reps. Two of the 17 sessions have too few, so that row would show an empty state rather than a number. Per hard rule #8, an empty-state component, not a dash.

Five June sessions have no weather at all, so heat cost is genuinely unknown for them — not zero. The row has to say so.

Hills is only half available. The laps table carries elevation gain (8 to 100 ft here) but no grade-adjusted pace, so hills can be reported as feet climbed but not as a seconds cost. That needs a flat_pace_sec_per_mile column alongside the heat-adjusted one you already have.
