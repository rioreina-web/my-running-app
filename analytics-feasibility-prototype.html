Finding four · controlling for pace

You were right, and it matters more than I expected.

The drift numbers above use the efficiency ratio — speed divided by heart rate, second half against first. That looks like it controls for pace, but it assumes heart rate scales linearly with speed. It doesn't. The HR–speed curve steepens as you go faster, so on any run where the second half was quicker the ratio hands you credit for speed you actually paid for in heartbeats, and drift comes out too low.

So I recomputed it without that assumption. Bin every second of every run into 0.1 m/s speed buckets, then compare heart rate between halves only within the same bucket — like against like, no model, no functional form. On average 80% of the theoretically pairable samples match, so this isn't a thin slice.

The third column is a fuller model — heart rate regressed on speed, speed squared, elapsed time and gradient, per run, where the elapsed-time coefficient is pace-controlled drift in bpm per hour. It agrees on direction everywhere but reads higher (+6.38% mean, 5.4 bpm/hr). It's an upper bound, not the answer: a single linear time term absorbs late-run curvature and then extrapolates it across the whole duration. Aug 1 at +20% is that failure mode, not a real reading. Speed and time are only weakly correlated within your runs (|r| < 0.35 on all sixteen), so collinearity isn't what's driving the gap.

Ship the speed-matched number. It makes no assumption about the shape of the HR–pace curve, and it degrades honestly — when a run has too little speed overlap to compare, coverage drops and you can say so instead of guessing.

Revised seasonal read: +0.96% spring, +4.43% June onward. The summer rise is bigger than the ratio method suggested, though dew point still explains most of it.
