The hero grid

y = day of week (Mon→Sun). x = week. One dot per quality session, on the day it happened. Size = effort (quality load). Colour = the pace of its fast segments, on the blue depth ramp. Weekend rows carry a faint wash so the week reads as a shape.

What it shows that nothing else does: the Tuesday band is a threshold habit, the Thursday band is intervals, the Sunday band is long-run marathon-pace work. That rhythm is invisible in any weekly-total chart. Two clean vertical gaps in the six-month view are the holiday and the work trip.

Open rings are real work that didn't clear the effort floor — strides, a stray surge. Visible, never weighted like a session.

The supporting tracks

Volume — weekly miles. The darker cap inside each bar is quality miles, so intensity rides inside volume instead of needing its own chart.

ACWR — acute (this week) ÷ chronic (trailing 4-week mean), computed from the same miles. The gray band is 0.8–1.3. Above it the dot goes coral (ramping fast); below it the dot goes hollow (detraining, not danger).

Recovery — one signal at a time (HRV deviation from your own baseline, sleep hours, or rest days). Three units can't share one axis honestly, so they take turns.

Mood — dominant voice-log mood, on the warm mood palette.

Niggles — a coral ring on any week a body part was mentioned. Surfaced verbatim in the scrub readout, never interpreted.

Design calls worth arguing with

Day-of-week beats pace as the y-axis. Earlier versions plotted sessions by their pace. That reads as a scatter you have to decode. Day-of-week is a categorical axis the athlete already carries in her head — she knows Tuesday is threshold day — so the grid needs no decoding at all, and it surfaces a pattern (the weekly rhythm, and when it breaks) that a pace scatter can't.

Pace moved from position to colour. Losing the pace axis costs precision, and the trade is worth it: colour is enough to see what kind of work a session was, and exact paces belong in the scrub readout, where they're stated as numbers rather than estimated off an axis. The keySessions.ts invariant — "compare like with like" — is respected by construction here, because nothing draws a line between sessions of different zones at all.

No dual axes, ever. Each supporting track is its own band with its own scale, sharing one x. Two measures never fight over one y.

Coral is spent twice, deliberately. ACWR out of zone and niggles — both genuine alerts. Note the shipped Swift chart still paints key-session pace coral, which contradicts the 2026-07-03 three-palette rule (blue = pace, coral = alert). This prototype moves pace to blue.

Recovery takes turns. HRV in ms, sleep in hours and rest in days can't honestly share a band. A sub-toggle is more honest than three overlaid scales.

Default on: volume, ACWR, pace, mood, niggles. Default off: key sessions, recovery. All seven on is a legitimate power-user state; it just isn't the 5-second view.

Empty weeks stay empty. A week with no quality session has no dot and reads "No key session" in the readout — never an em-dash, per hard rule #8.

Does the grid survive your real data?

Partly. Flip the dataset toggle above the phone and compare — this is the honest test, and it changes the verdict.

Sample data (invented): 59 sessions, 32% of the grid full, a clean three-band rhythm. That rhythm was a pattern I authored into the data and then congratulated the chart for revealing. Not evidence.

Your real data: 19 sessions, 10% of the grid full. Monday and Thursday are empty all six months. The first eight weeks are entirely blank — there's no lap data before 23 March, so nothing can be classified. At 6 MO the hero is mostly whitespace.

But the core premise holds at 12 weeks. Tuesday is a genuine band (10 of your 19 sessions), Wednesday is a real second band, and Friday is where the strides live. That's a legible rhythm and it is worth showing. The grid is right — the range is wrong. 12 wk should be the default, and 6 MO probably shouldn't offer the grid at all.

Real data broke two things the sample never would have. Your ACWR hits 1.77 and 1.57 — the band was hard-clamped at 1.55, so both spikes pinned to the top edge and the value label landed on top of the track title. Domain is adaptive now. Your sample-data ACWR never left 0.66–1.32, so neither bug could appear.

Recovery has no data at all. No HRV, sleep or rest anywhere in the schema. On the real dataset that track renders its empty state instead of numbers, and the chip switches itself off.

The effort score, calibrated on real data

Quality load = Σ(work-bout seconds × zone weight) ÷ 60. The weights are the canonical 1–5 scale already in workoutSegmentation.ts (mp 2.5 → mile 5.0). The score is the sum, not the average — averaging over the whole workout is the retired intensity_score that quality-volume.ts documents as broken.

Checked against 23 real sessions in prod. Ranked by load, the top of the list is 6×1mi @ 5:12 (103), a 6.2mi 10K effort (102), a ten-rep 1K set (101) and 9×1K @ 5:08 (100). The bottom is 3×300m (11.7), 3×200m (6.4) and 2×200m (5.4).

The floor needs no tuning. Strides top out at 13.1; the smallest genuine session is 42.1; nothing lands in between. Any floor from 15 to 40 keeps the same 19 of 23 and cuts the same 4 — all four unambiguously strides. Zero real sessions lost.

But the weights barely matter here. Quality load correlates 0.965 with plain work-duration across those sessions — the mp→mile spread is only 2×, while work duration spans 30×. For the gate specifically, "≥ 5 minutes of work" gives the identical 19/4 split with no weight table to maintain. The weights earn their keep for dot size, where the 1.9× spread in load-per-minute is a real, visible difference.

The number stays hidden. Quality load drives dot size and the anchor-session pick, and nothing else. The scrub readout describes the week in minutes of work — a figure you can check against your own watch — not in load units, which you can't. A synthetic score printed as fact is exactly the kind of unfalsifiable number the voice guide tells us not to ship.

Two things worth knowing. About half of all quality load sits within 4 s/mi of a zone boundary, so a new race anchor can re-bracket it — if that bites, a continuous intensity factor (IF⁴) ranks these same sessions at r = 0.994 with no brackets to straddle. And textbook rTSS (IF²) would be worse than what you have: r = 0.988 with duration, even more volume-dominated. The 2026-06-12 scale beats the industry standard on your data.

About the sample data

26 weeks, reshaped from TrendsSampleData so the new tracks have something to say. The April dip is a work trip (24 mi, 3 rest days), the ramp back out of it pushes ACWR to 1.32 in the week of May 5 — which is also the week HRV drops 9 ms, sleep falls to 6.0 h, mood reads struggling, and the right achilles gets mentioned for the first time. That single vertical slice is the whole product thesis.

ACWR is computed live from the mileage series (acute ÷ trailing 4-week mean), not hand-authored, so it stays honest if you edit the miles.
