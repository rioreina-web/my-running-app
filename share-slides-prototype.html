What goes out.

Exported at 3× from the same views drawn on screen. The cover carries the stacked wordmark; every slide after it carries only the drip.

The memo is the engine

The system's rule is that the interface supplies facts and the athlete supplies feeling, in their own transcribed words. That makes a share slide almost impossible to write without the memo — and trivially good with it. Six seconds of rambling contains more usable type than any generator: legs were absolute bricks is not a sentence a machine would produce, which is exactly why it's worth printing.

Verbatim spans, never paraphrase

A candidate must be a contiguous run of words from the transcript, 3–9 words, filler trimmed at the edges only. It may never contain a number — the app owns numbers, and a mis-transcribed "fifteen" contradicting a measured 14.99 on a public post is the one failure that can't be walked back. Same discipline as no invented splits.

Italic mono is the athlete

The system draws its sharpest line between the person and the machine by posture, not colour: italic mono is the athlete, roman mono is the machine. A pulled phrase is a transcript span, so it stays in JetBrains Mono italic even at 30px — where it reads as raw tape, which is what it is. The display-italic alternative is in the rail; look at both.

No headline editorialises

The cover says 15 miles, long run. — not "The last 3 miles were the fastest." An app writing poetry about someone else's run is the failure mode the copy rules exist to prevent. Everything with a feeling in it on these slides came out of the athlete's mouth.

The trace as a field, not a picture

On the route slide the trace is drawn oversized and bled off two edges at low opacity, so it becomes the page's texture rather than a map to be read. The pace colour stays honest — it's the zone-anchored ramp from pv-data.jsx — but nobody is reading a route at that scale. They're reading four words on a surface no other app can generate.

Two ways onto the photograph

Type over a darkened duotone, or type in a solid ink band at the foot with the photograph left bright. Direction I has no gradients and no shadows, so a scrim was never available — both options are honest print moves. The band protects the photograph; the overlay makes a poster. The rail switches them.
