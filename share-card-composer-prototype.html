What lands in the feed

Both themes, side by side.

Paper is the shipped system — a page torn out of a printed running log, which is what makes it read as not another Strava screenshot in a feed. Ink is the same page inverted for phones held at night; the pace ramp flips with it.

Why a magazine, not a card

Every running app exports a map with numbers on it, and they are interchangeable because they inherit the same basemap and the same system font. A magazine has furniture — masthead, issue number, running head, department, folio, drop cap, figure caption, byline. None of it is decoration; each piece tells the reader what kind of object they are holding. That furniture is what can't be copied in a sprint, because copying it means owning a type system first.

The cover is not a photograph

The system's hardest rule is no images as background, ever. So the cover doesn't put type over a sunrise — the art sits in a field of paper with the masthead above it and the cover line below, the way an essay cover works. The route is the art because it is the one mark unique to this run; the photograph gets its own page, in Dispatch.

The coral budget, spent once a page

Building this cost coral three times over: department labels, the fastest split's bar, and the drop cap all wanted it. Two had to go. Department labels fell to ink-2, and the fastest bar went back to the deep end of the ramp — the ramp already says "fastest", so coral there was saying it twice. The drop cap kept it, because the system names a coloured capital as the reason coral exists.

Pace colour on a dark page

The shipped ramp deepens pale sky to navy — intensity read as depth, which works on warm paper. On ink, navy disappears and the fastest miles vanish with it. The ink ramp inverts the luminance: dim slate for easy, near-white sky for mile. Same semantic, opposite direction — light is the only axis a black field has.

The running order is fixed

Pages can be added and dropped; they cannot be reordered. A magazine's running order is an editorial decision, not a reader preference, and the sequence — cover, the numbers, dispatch — is the argument the issue makes: here is the run, here is what it did, here is what it felt like. Shuffling it produces a worse magazine every time.

Two pages are off by default

The Ledger is the most impressive page and the least legible in a feed — nine rep rows of monospace at 1080 px is a photograph of a spreadsheet. It earns its place on an interval day. The Last Word gives the coach note a home for athletes who want it, and is a distraction for the ones who don't. Turn them on to see them.
