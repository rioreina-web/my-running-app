Post Run Drip · Trends · 27 July 2026

Trends needs a spine, not more charts.

Every piece in Trends today is well made. The problem is that they are filed by data type — effort, fitness, signal, mood, niggles, pace — so nothing tells you what to read first, and a second tab had to be invented to hold the overflow. This is a proposal for one order, two new surfaces, and three things to delete. Built on 84 real days and 20 real key sessions, 4 May – 26 July 2026.

The diagnosis

A segmented control (EFFORT / FITNESS / SIGNAL), a “GO DEEPER” section, two accordions, a drill-down arrow, and a whole second tab called TRENDS 2. Six top-level choices before the athlete has been told a single thing. The categories are honest descriptions of where the numbers came from, which is exactly why they don’t work as navigation — nobody opens an app asking “show me my signal.”

A training block only ever answers three questions, and they come in a fixed order: am I doing the work, is it landing, and can I keep going. Load, response, capacity. Trends should be those three, in that order, and nothing else.

The good news is that almost everything already exists. The reorganisation costs one deletion and two new surfaces — the two you named.

What gets deleted: the EFFORT / FITNESS / SIGNAL segmented control, the TRENDS 2 tab, and the standalone MOOD and NIGGLES accordions. Mood and niggles aren’t a category of their own — they are how you tell whether the body is absorbing the work. They belong inside Holding Up, and putting them there is what makes that surface worth opening. Head-to-head compare stops being a top-level destination and becomes what it always was: what you get when you tap a session.

The prototype

Three screens, real data.

Tap a row in the ledger to open the head-to-head against the last session of the same kind. Tap a week in Holding Up to scrub it. Every number is computed from your own Strava and Supabase data — nothing here is sample.

The call

Key Sessions should be the Ledger.

You explored three directions. I’d ship A — The Ledger, with the session grid you applied this morning as its header and exactly one thing grafted in from B. Here is why, and the reasons are in your data rather than in taste.

B — The Fitness Signal — would draw flat lines and imply nothing happened

B’s own note says it needs six sessions of a kind before a trend means anything. Your real counts are intervals 7, long 11, threshold 2 — threshold is unreadable and will stay that way. Worse, B’s headline signals barely move on twelve weeks of real work: efficiency trends +0.01 for intervals and −0.01 for long runs; drift −0.03 and −0.13. Those are four charts that cost real engineering and tell an athlete who trained hard for three months that nothing changed. That is not a neutral outcome — it is actively discouraging, and it isn’t even true.

C — Execution & Load — is already two screens you own

C’s own note concedes it “probably splits into Session detail + a Load tile.” Agreed — and both halves already have homes in this structure. The week-behind-it half (volume, ACWR, mood on key days) is The Work and Holding Up. The per-rep trajectory and grade half is session detail, one tap below the ledger. C isn’t a rejected direction so much as a correct observation that got filed as a screen.

A wins because you already built its payload

The head-to-head compare in your screenshots — Output against Cost, fast volume, density, average rest, rep recovery — is Direction A’s answer, and it is the best-looking surface of the four. It doesn’t need building. It needs a front door, and the session grid is that door. A also degrades honestly: with one session it’s a row, with five it’s a list, with twenty it’s a block. B is meaningless below six and C is meaningless without lap data.

Grid answers when. Ledger answers what, and did it hold. Tap answers was it better than last time. One surface, three zoom levels, and the deepest one already ships.

The one thing to take from B: heat-neutral pace — as a column, not a screen

This is the most valuable idea in all three directions and it costs a single number per row. Across your block the estimated heat penalty rose from 3.3% in early May to 5.0% by late July. Raw pace says your long runs went 6:52 → 6:51, i.e. nowhere. Heat-neutral says 6:38 → 6:31 — seven seconds a mile of real improvement that the thermometer was eating. Intervals tell the honest opposite story: 5:17 → 5:28 raw looks like a collapse, 5:07 → 5:11 heat-neutral is a small genuine regression. Both readings are more useful than the raw number alone, and neither needs a chart of its own.

What Recovery can honestly be

There is no biometric substrate. Your HealthKit code reads heart rate, distance, active energy and step count — no HRV, no sleep, no resting heart rate. The voice pipeline is specified to extract sleep, fatigue and stress, but over 255 logs in 180 days it landed sleep 7 times, fatigue 9 and stress 0; felt_rpe is an empty column. A readiness score built on that would be fiction.

So Holding Up is built from things with complete coverage, because they come from the calendar and the splits rather than a sensor: how far apart the hard days sit, how often you stop, how much the week varies, and whether easy days are staying easy. The strongest finding in your block came out of that: the hard days are well handled — median four days apart, never back to back, thirteen of twenty graded held or better — while five of twelve weeks cross the sameness threshold and every one of those five had zero days off. The risk in this block is not intensity. It is that nothing ever changes.

The reserved empty slot matters too. When HRV, sleep and resting heart rate do arrive, they drop into a surface whose argument already holds without them, instead of becoming the surface.

The numbers, defined
Key sessionA run whose fast work clears the quality-load floor — the same gate you shipped this morning. It cuts exactly one thing from the 21 candidates: the 8×200 stride set on 24 July, 1.6 km of fast running. Twenty real sessions remain.
Heat-neutral paceKey pace with the temperature and dew-point penalty removed: (temp°F + dew°F − 100) × 0.1%, capped at 10%. Asks every session the same question regardless of season.
GradeBack half against front half of the fast work. Negative split, held, eased, faded. Your twenty: 8 negative split, 5 held, 5 eased, 2 faded.
SamenessWeekly mean daily mileage divided by its standard deviation — Foster monotony. Flag line at 2.0. Never shown to the athlete as a number with a name; shown as bar length with the definition on tap.
Metres per heartbeatDistance covered per beat on easy days only. Rising means the same easy pace costs less. Weekly mean over easy days with both pace and heart rate present.
Provenance

Every figure on this page is computed from your own data: 20 key sessions with lap and heart-rate detail from Strava, 4 May – 26 July 2026, and 84 consecutive days of mile splits with heart rate from training_logs.pace_segments in RunningAppMVP2. Volume, rest days, sameness, easy-day heart rate and the metres-per-beat series were derived here and are reproducible from those two sources.

Estimated: temperature and dew point come from station climatology for the session date and start hour — the archive weather API isn’t reachable from this sandbox. Production reads real per-session weather from the existing fetch-workout-weather edge function, so the heat-neutral figures will shift slightly and the direction will not.

Not sample, not invented: nothing on this page is placeholder. Where data genuinely doesn’t exist — sleep, HRV, resting heart rate — the surface shows an empty slot and says so.
