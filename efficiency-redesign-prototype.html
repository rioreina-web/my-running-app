Efficiency card — three ways to make it readable

Same underlying maths. The question each one has to answer in under three seconds is “is my engine getting better?” Numbers below are illustrative, in the shape your real data takes.

Option A · pace at a fixed heart rate
Efficiency
At 160 bpm you’re running 6:02 a mile.
In April it was 6:11.
Same heartbeat, nine seconds a mile faster.
One dot a month · faster is lower
What this means›

Why this one. No new vocabulary. Runners already think in pace and heart rate, so the sentence needs no explanation — and “nine seconds a mile faster at the same effort” is the actual finding, stated in the units you feel. The index still runs underneath; it just never shows its face.

Option B · one number, one direction
Efficiency · last 28 days
+3%
More ground per heartbeat
Your heart is buying more distance than it did in the spring.
What the data says
Six sessions in the last four weeks, all measured against cool-weather equivalents.
The sessions behind this›

Why this one. The percentage is a real change against your past self, not a score that sits at 100 forever. One number, one arrow, one sentence — and the dotted line is simply “spring”, not a curve that needs defining.

Option C · the sentence alone
Efficiency
Holding steady.
Through an Austin summer, at 60–70 miles a week — which is its own kind of progress.
Apr → Aug · nothing lost to the heat
Show me the numbers›

Why this one. The most ruthless version: a verdict in two words, a line whose only job is to show it isn’t falling, and every figure — zones, heat cost, metres per beat, what got excluded — behind one tap. Fits the “observation, never prescription” rule exactly.

What all three delete from the card you saw

  • The word “index”, and “180-day speed-and-heat curve”. Internal machinery, not reader-facing language.
  • The 28-dot scatter. It was showing per-session noise around a flat line. A monthly line answers the question; the cloud never did.
  • “1.81 m/beat”, “−0.6% heat cost per step”, the four-zone number row. Real figures, wrong surface — they belong behind the tap, for the day you want them.
  • “22 not counted.” Nearly half your sessions have no heart rate. That deserves its own honest line somewhere, not a footnote that reads like the card is broken.
  • The number that couldn’t move. Scoring the last 28 days against a baseline containing those same days pins the headline near 100 forever. Fixed by baselining on the prior window — a real 5% gain then reads as 5%, not 4.2%.
