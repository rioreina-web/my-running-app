# Post Run Drip — Beta Launch Plan

*Closed, high-touch beta · Target 25–50 runners · Opens Mon Jul 13, 2026 · Budget under $500*

---

## The one-line version

Hand-pick 25–50 self-coached runners from your own network and a few local run clubs, get them running the app for 3–4 weeks, and structure the whole thing so you come out the other side knowing one thing for sure: **is this app actually smart, or does it just look smart?**

This is not a growth campaign. It's a controlled experiment dressed up as a launch. Every choice below optimizes for *learning*, not for sign-up numbers.

---

## 1. Campaign overview

**Campaign name:** Post Run Drip — Founding Runners Beta

**Summary:** A small, invite-only beta that recruits committed runners to test whether the app's coaching observations are genuinely useful — and to give you the raw feedback to make v1 worth shipping.

**Primary objective:** Recruit 25–50 active beta testers who each log at least 3 weeks of real training, and collect structured feedback on (a) whether the features work and (b) whether the AI's reads feel accurate and worth reading.

**Specific, measurable goal:**
> By **August 10, 2026** (4 weeks after launch), have **30+ runners onboarded**, **15+ still actively logging in week 3**, and **at least 20 pieces of structured feedback** on the AI's coaching quality.

**Secondary objectives:**
- Find the 3–5 things that break, confuse, or feel wrong — before strangers ever see them.
- Identify 5–10 "founding runners" who love it enough to become references / word-of-mouth at public launch.
- Pressure-test the core bet: that fusing voice logs + training data into *observations* (not prescriptions) is something runners actually want.

## 2. Target audience

You picked the right audience for a beta: **people you can talk to directly.** A 25–50 person beta lives or dies on engagement, and engagement comes from relationship, not reach.

**Primary segment — "Maya" runners in your network.**
Self-coached endurance runners who already take training somewhat seriously: they track their runs, have a race on the calendar or a goal pace in mind, and journal or at least think about how training is *feeling*, not just the numbers. They're the people who'd actually open a "how's my training going?" app.

> *A self-coached runner training 25–50 miles/week for a half or full marathon, who logs runs on Strava/Garmin, has opinions about their training, and is curious enough to try a rough app and tell you the truth about it.*

**Secondary segment — local run club members.**
Slightly warmer-than-cold: people who know you or know someone who knows you. Run clubs are gold for a beta because the members are pre-qualified (they run consistently) and socially accountable (they'll show up if a real person asked them).

**What they care about (and what to lead with):**
- Honest feedback on their training without paying for a coach.
- Feeling *seen* — "this thing noticed something true about my running."
- Being early to something a friend is building (people love this more than you'd expect).

**What they are NOT:**
- Not strangers on the App Store. Don't try to reach them yet.
- Not casual "couch to 5k" beginners — they won't generate enough data to test the smart features.

**Buying stage:** Pre-product. They're not "buying" anything — they're doing you a favor and getting early access. Frame accordingly (see messaging).

## 3. Key messages

**Core message:**
> *"I built a running app that reads your training like a coach would — and I want 30 runners I trust to tell me if it's any good."*

The honesty is the hook. You're not selling a finished product; you're inviting people into something. That's a stronger ask at this stage than any feature list.

**Supporting messages (use the one that fits the person):**

1. **It's a coach's eye, not another dashboard.** *"Most apps just show you charts. This one reads your runs and your voice notes together and tells you what it notices — like a coach glancing at your week."* — Proof: the voice-log + training-data fusion is the actual differentiator.

2. **It observes, it never bosses you around.** *"It won't tell you to rest or diagnose an injury. It surfaces what's there and leaves the call to you."* — Proof: this is a genuine design principle, and self-coached runners are wary of apps that pretend to be a doctor.

3. **You'd be one of ~30 founding testers.** *"This is small and hands-on. Your feedback literally shapes what ships."* — Proof: 25–50 cap makes it real and a little exclusive.

4. **Five minutes to start, then just run.** *"Connect your runs, talk to it after a few sessions, and tell me what lands and what doesn't."* — Proof: low friction lowers the bar to a yes.

**Tone:** Warm, plain, founder-to-friend. No marketing gloss — your audience is people who know you, and over-polishing will read as fake. Match the product's own voice: restraint, honesty, one bit of warmth.

## 4. Channel strategy

With a sub-$500 budget and a personal-network audience, this is **100% owned + earned, zero paid.** Spend money only on things that remove friction, not on reach.

| Channel | Why it fits | Format | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1:1 direct messages** | Your highest-conversion channel by far. A personal "would you try this?" converts 10x better than any post. | Text/WhatsApp/DM, ~8–12 sentences, personalized | Medium |
| **Local run club asks** | Pre-qualified runners + social accountability. One captain's intro reaches 20–50 people warmly. | Short pitch to club organizer + a message they can forward | Medium |
| **Personal social post (organic)** | Catches the warm-but-not-close tier — acquaintances who'd raise their hand. | One honest IG/Strava/Facebook post + story | Low |
| **Waitlist / interest landing page** | A single link to send everywhere; collects names so you control the invite flow. | One-page site (free tier tool) | Low–Medium |
| **Email follow-up to sign-ups** | Onboards testers and runs the weekly feedback rhythm. | Plain-text emails, 4 over the beta | Low |

**Where the $500 could go (only if needed):**
- Domain + simple landing page / form tool: ~$15–40 (Carrd, Typeform free tier, or a Google Form for $0).
- A small thank-you for testers who finish: e.g. $10–15 gift cards or branded stickers/socks for your most active 10 → ~$150–300. *This is the highest-leverage spend* — it dramatically lifts week-3 retention and goodwill.
- Keep ~$100 in reserve for anything that unblocks a tester (e.g. a TestFlight issue, a quick design asset).

> **Don't spend on ads.** At 25–50 testers from your own network, paid reach is wasted money and brings in low-quality, low-engagement strangers — the opposite of what a beta needs.

**Hard dependencies (do these in order):**
1. TestFlight build stable → before *any* invite goes out.
2. Landing page / form live → before the social post (it's the link).
3. Onboarding email written → before launch week.
4. 2–3 closest people have tested → before the wider list gets invited.

**Invite in waves, not all at once.** Send 10 invites, watch what breaks, fix it, send the next 15. A bug that hits all 50 people on day one is a wasted beta; the same bug caught at person #8 is a Tuesday.

## 7. Success metrics

Because the goal is *learning*, the metrics are about engagement and signal quality — not vanity sign-ups.

**Primary KPI:**
- **Active week-3 loggers:** 15+ testers still logging real runs in week 3. *This is the number that matters most* — it means the app held attention long enough for the smart features to be judged fairly.

**Secondary KPIs:**

| Metric | Target | How to track |
|---|---|---|
| Onboarded testers | 30+ | TestFlight installs + first run logged |
| Activation (logged ≥1 run + 1 voice note) | 70% of installs | In-app / your DB |
| Coach Read generated per tester | 2+ | Your backend (`coachable_moments` / read events) |
| "Did it notice something true?" — yes rate | 60%+ say yes | Weekly form |
| Structured feedback responses | 20+ | Feedback form |
| Tester interviews completed | 5–8 | Calendar |

**Reporting cadence:** Check the engagement numbers **weekly** (a simple spreadsheet is fine). Do a full synthesis at the end of week 4.

> **The single question to answer:** *When the app told a runner something about their training, was it right, and did they care?* If yes for most testers — you have a real product. If no — you've learned exactly what to fix before spending a dollar on public launch.

## 8. Testing whether it's "actually a smart app"

This is your stated goal, so it gets its own section. "Is it smart?" isn't one question — it's three, and you need to test each separately.

**A. Is it accurate?** Do the numbers and observations match reality?
- Have each tester compare one Coach Read against what they *know* about their week. Ask: *"Anything in here factually wrong?"*
- Watch the high-stakes outputs: pace zones, fitness range, niggle mentions. A wrong pace zone is worse than no pace zone.

**B. Is it insightful?** Does it notice things a runner wouldn't have noticed themselves?
- The key question on every form: *"Did it point out anything you hadn't already realized?"* A smart app earns its place by surfacing the non-obvious — a fatigue trend, a niggle cluster, a pace creeping up.
- If testers say "it just told me what I already knew," that's the most important finding you can get.

**C. Is it trustworthy?** Does it stay in its lane and feel honest?
- Check that it never crosses into diagnosing injuries or prescribing rest (your own product principle). Ask testers if anything felt like overreach.
- Check the honesty of predictions — ranges with confidence, never fake-precise single numbers. Ask if any number felt made-up.

**Make feedback comparable — give everyone the same 3 tasks:**
1. Log at least 4 runs and 2 voice notes over 2 weeks.
2. Generate a Coach Read and rate it 1–5 on "accurate" and "useful."
3. Tell me one thing it got right and one thing it got wrong.

Same tasks → you can actually compare across 30 people instead of 30 one-off opinions.

## 9. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Not enough data → smart features can't fire.** Testers log 1–2 runs and the AI has nothing to read. | High | Set the expectation up front: "this needs ~2 weeks of runs to show its value." Recruit runners who already run 3+×/week. Personally nudge low-loggers in week 1. |
| **Drop-off after install.** People install, never log, quietly vanish. | High | Wave-based invites + personal 1:1 follow-up. A small finisher reward. Make week-1 onboarding dead simple. |
| **A bug burns a tester.** First impression ruined by a crash or wrong number. | Medium | Soft-test with 2–3 people first. Invite in waves. Fix fast between waves. |
| **Feedback is vague** ("it's cool!") and you learn nothing. | Medium | Structured form + the same 3 tasks for everyone + 5–8 real interviews. Specific questions beat open praise. |
| **TestFlight friction** (Apple's invite flow confuses non-techy testers). | Medium | Write a 3-step "how to install" with screenshots. Be on call during launch week. |
